# Enterprise production cases: documented parallel AI agent fleets (state: November 2026)

Research date: 2026-11-14. Rating scale used throughout: **A** = first-hand technical write-up with numbers, or audited figure. **B** = official claim without technical detail, or credible independent reporting. **C** = marketing claim or unverified.

Verification note for the report writer: where a line says "(fetched)", I read the page itself in this session. Where it says "(search summary only)", the figure comes from a search-engine summary of that page and I did not open the page; treat those one notch weaker. Several 2026 events post-date my training data, so I relied on the sources, not on prior knowledge.

## Key Question 1: Coding-agent fleets inside companies — what is documented?

### Takeaway
The best-documented fleets (Stripe, Ramp, Spotify, Shopify, Vercel, OpenAI, Anthropic) all publish first-hand numbers, and all describe the same shape: many short, isolated, one-task agent runs that end in a pull request a human reviews. No company found runs a self-organising "swarm" in production. The experiments with hundreds of concurrent agents (Cursor) or 16 peer agents (Anthropic's C compiler) are research runs, not production systems, and neither discloses business value. Cost is almost never disclosed; the only hard cost figures are from Anthropic's two experiments (just under $20,000 and roughly $18,000).

### Cited Findings

#### Stripe — "Minions" (Rating A)
- Part 1 (fetched), 9 Feb 2026, by Alistair Gray: "Over a thousand pull requests merged each week at Stripe are completely minion-produced"; they are human-reviewed but "contain no human-written code". — [Stripe Dev Blog, Part 1](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents)
- Part 2 (fetched), 19 Feb 2026: "Over 1,300 Stripe pull requests (up from 1,000 as of Part 1) merged each week are completely minion-produced". — [Stripe Dev Blog, Part 2](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2)
- Invocation: most commonly by tagging a Slack app in a thread; also CLI, web, and buttons inside internal tools (docs platform, feature-flag platform, ticketing UI; CI creates flaky-test tickets with a "start a minion" button). Engineers "frequently" spin up several minions in parallel, especially on call. No per-engineer number is given. — [Stripe Dev Blog, Part 1](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents)
- Sandboxing: each minion runs on a standard developer "devbox" (AWS EC2, pre-warmed pool, target ready within 10 seconds). Devboxes sit in the QA environment with no access to real user data, production services or arbitrary network egress, so minions run with full permissions and no confirmation prompts; a mistake is limited to one devbox. — [Stripe Dev Blog, Part 2](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2)
- Orchestration: "blueprints" are code-defined workflows that mix deterministic nodes (run linters, push the branch; no LLM) with agent nodes (implement the task, fix CI failures). Stripe says handling predictable steps deterministically saves tokens and CI cost. — [Stripe Dev Blog, Part 2](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2)
- Harness and tools: the agent loop is a fork of Block's open-source goose (forked late 2024), stripped of human-facing features such as interruptibility. A central MCP server, "Toolshed", holds "nearly 500 MCP tools" (more than 400 in Part 1) and serves "hundreds of different agents"; minions get a small curated subset by default. — [Stripe Dev Blog, Part 2](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2)
- Feedback and review gates: local lint loop (under five seconds), then at most two full CI rounds against a battery of "over three million" tests; after the second push "we send the branch back to its human operator for manual scrutiny". Stripe cites "diminishing marginal returns" from more loops. A second Stripe engineer reviews the PR. — [Stripe Dev Blog, Part 2](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2); [Part 1](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents)
- Why in-house: an uncommon stack (Ruby with Sorbet, many homegrown libraries) and stakes ("well over $1 trillion per year of payment volume"). — [Stripe Dev Blog, Part 1](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents)
- Task type: small, routine issues such as flaky tests and on-call fixes. — [Stripe Dev Blog, Part 1](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents)

#### Ramp — "Inspect" (Rating A for architecture; share figures are first-hand plus independent reporting)
- Original engineering post (fetched; page carries no date, InfoQ covered it in January 2026): "~30% of all pull requests merged to our frontend and backend repos are written by Inspect", reached within "a couple months". — [Ramp Builders](https://builders.ramp.com/post/why-we-built-our-background-agent); [InfoQ, Jan 2026](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/01/ramp-coding-agent-platform)
- Architecture: each session runs in a sandboxed VM on Modal with the full dev stack (Vite, Postgres, Temporal); images are rebuilt and snapshotted every 30 minutes, so a session starts at most 30 minutes behind the repo. The agent is OpenCode (chosen because it is "structured as a server first"). Session state lives in Cloudflare Durable Objects with one SQLite database per session. Agents can spawn child sessions for cross-repo research or to split work into smaller PRs. Sessions are multiplayer. Clients: Slack (a fast model classifies the target repo), web (hosted VS Code, streamed desktop), Chrome extension. — [Ramp Builders](https://builders.ramp.com/post/why-we-built-our-background-agent)
- Verification: the agent runs tests, reads telemetry, queries feature flags, and for frontend work checks its change visually and attaches before/after screenshots. PRs are opened with the requesting user's GitHub token so nobody can approve their own change. — [Ramp Builders](https://builders.ramp.com/post/why-we-built-our-background-agent)
- Independent deep-dive (fetched, partly paywalled), 25 Aug 2026: "75% of all merged PRs come from Inspect sessions"; "one million total sessions" crossed in July 2026; the Inspect team is "5.5 people"; "150+ engineers at Ramp" have contributed to it; "more than 80% of Inspect is written in Inspect sessions"; engineers "have built more than 200 agents running on top of the Inspect platform"; environments spin up in "under 5 seconds". — [The Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/why-ramp-built-inspect)
- Latest official figure (fetched), 27 Oct 2026, by co-founder Karim Atiyeh: "Inspect now accounts for 74% of merged PRs" in the past month; "99% of our engineering, design, and product teams used Inspect"; builders ship 6x as much code per person as a year earlier. This post also launches Inspect as an external product (early access), so it is partly a sales document. — [Ramp blog](https://ramp.com/blog/introducing-inspect)
- Conflict: Ramp's own post says ~30% while The Pragmatic Engineer says "around 60% of PRs at Ramp were authored by Inspect" by January 2026, and Modal's case study reported over half in February 2026 (search summary only). The wording differs ("written by Inspect" vs "come from Inspect sessions"), and the undated Ramp post may describe an earlier month. — [Ramp Builders](https://builders.ramp.com/post/why-we-built-our-background-agent); [The Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/why-ramp-built-inspect); [Modal](https://modal.com/blog/how-ramp-built-a-full-context-background-coding-agent-on-modal)

#### Spotify — "Honk" and Fleet Management (Rating A)
- Nov 2025 (fetched; older data): "our agents have generated more than 1,500 pull requests that teams across Spotify have merged"; "Since mid-2024, around half of Spotify's pull requests have been automated by this system" (Fleet Management overall, not the LLM agent alone); "a total time saving of 60–90% compared to writing the code by hand". — [Spotify Engineering, 6 Nov 2025](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/11/spotifys-background-coding-agent-part-1)
- QCon London, reported 18 Mar 2026 (fetched): Honk reached "1,000 merged pull requests every 10 days", against 1,000 in three months six months earlier. Migration timelines fell "from nearly a year to under a week for 70% of the fleet"; "the remaining 30% proved extremely difficult". Honk pushes a branch, triggers builds through a verification service, and "only creates pull requests after full validation". — [InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/03/spotify-honk-rewrite)
- June 2026 (fetched): more than 99% of engineers use AI coding tools weekly; PR frequency is up 76%; Fleet Management has merged more than 2.5 million automated maintenance PRs, "the vast majority auto-merged with no human in the loop"; Honk runs Claude through the Agent SDK inside Kubernetes pods; a separate system ("Fleetshift") picks targets, schedules and tracks progress while Honk only makes the change; a Java migration across backend services took three days. — [Spotify Engineering, 3 Jun 2026](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/6/code-with-claude-coding-is-no-longer-the-constraint)
- Oct 2026 (fetched): monthly merged changes rose from about 8,100 to 17,000 year over year in August 2026 (unit not explained); change volume outpaced some verification controls; an automated dependency upgrade passed all checks and failed in production; a 24 June content-pipeline incident delayed episodes for hours, but the review "found no material direct contribution from AI-authored code". — [InfoQ, 12 Oct 2026](https://infoq.com/news/2026/10/spotify-ai-quality-controls)

#### Shopify — "River" (Rating A)
- Fetched, 28 May 2026: "one in eight merged pull requests across Shopify is coauthored by it"; "3,536 River-coauthored pull requests merged in that window" (30 days); "59,918 River sessions happened in 5,170 Slack channels"; 7,000+ people used it; median session 19 minutes and 50 tool calls. Cost and concurrency are not disclosed. — [Shopify Engineering](https://shopify.engineering/under-the-river)
- Architecture: Slack-native, public channels only (no DMs, transcripts open to all staff). Underlying platform "Aquifer": durable Postgres-backed append-only session log, a disposable harness that runs outside the sandbox, disposable sandboxes for shell/edits/builds/tests, a credentials proxy, and Nix-built environment profiles. The same platform runs interactive mode, PR-review automation and CI/batch jobs. — [Shopify Engineering](https://shopify.engineering/under-the-river)
- "Coauthored" is weaker than "authored": the figure counts PRs River took part in. The post does not say who merges them. — [Shopify Engineering](https://shopify.engineering/under-the-river)

#### Vercel — agent review and auto-merge (Rating A; primary page found by search, direct fetch returned 404, so numbers are search summary only)
- 6 Apr 2026: in Vercel's largest monorepo (400+ PRs a week) an agent reviews and merges 58% of PRs with no human reviewer; average merge time fell from 29 hours to 10.9 hours. Changes touching authentication, payments, data integrity, security or infrastructure are always high risk and need a human; so do PRs with 100+ changed files and CODEOWNERS-protected paths. Vercel reports no increase in reverts. — [Vercel blog](https://vercel.com/blog/58-percent-of-prs-in-our-largest-monorepo-merge-without-human-review)

#### OpenAI — "harness engineering" and internal Codex use (Rating A for the experiment write-up, B for company-wide usage)
- Primary post by Ryan Lopopolo, 11 Feb 2026 (direct fetch returned HTTP 403; numbers below are from secondary summaries): an internal beta product of roughly one million lines of code built over five months with no manually written source code; about 1,500 PRs opened and merged; an average of 3.5 PRs per engineer per day. — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/); [The Neuron](https://www.theneuron.ai/explainer-articles/openais-harness-engineering-playbook-how-to-ship-1m-lines-of-code-without-writing-any/)
- Practices (fetched via InfoQ, 21 Feb 2026): a structured docs directory in the repo is the single source of truth for agents; cross-linked design docs are enforced by linters and CI; architectural boundaries are enforced by structural tests with a fixed dependency direction (Types → Config → Repo → Service → Runtime → UI); agents use logs, metrics and spans to reproduce bugs in isolated environments; Codex opens PRs and iterates until criteria are met. Humans design environments, specify intent and give structured feedback. — [InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/02/openai-harness-engineering-codex)
- Independent deep-dive (fetched, partly paywalled), 15 Sep 2026: non-engineering orgs "went from ~0% usage of Codex to 90% usage" in four months; "roughly a 10x increase in load on some systems" (CI/CD); "Instead of using one generic AI code reviewer, OpenAI spins off multiple agents" (domain specialists); "areas of the codebase can opt in to an agent that will auto-approve low risk PRs"; a human approves each change for production, after which "it is assigned its own agent" for deploy monitoring; the incident agent Sevbot "determines possible mitigations (but never executes any)". — [The Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/openai-software-factory)
- Company claim via secondary press (search summary only; Rating C): 92% of OpenAI engineers use Codex and complete about 70% more PRs a week. — [Analytics India Magazine](https://analyticsindiamag.com/ai-news-updates/almost-all-new-code-written-at-openai-today-is-from-codex-users/)

#### Anthropic — C compiler experiment and internal Claude Code share (Rating A for the experiment; A-/B for the 80% figure)
- C compiler (fetched), 5 Feb 2026, by Nicholas Carlini: 16 parallel Claude instances, nearly 2,000 Claude Code sessions over about two weeks, about 2 billion input and 140 million output tokens, "just under $20,000", output a roughly 100,000-line Rust compiler that "can build Linux 6.9 on x86, ARM, and RISC-V". — [Anthropic Engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)
- Design: a bash `while true` loop restarts a fresh session each time one ends; each agent has its own Docker container and clones a shared bare git repo; an agent claims a task by writing a lock file to `current_tasks/` and git conflicts force the loser to pick another task. "I don't use an orchestration agent." Parallelism on the kernel only worked after using GCC as an oracle so each agent could work on a different failing file. Some agents had fixed specialist roles (deduplication, performance, docs). — [Anthropic Engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)
- Stated limits: no 16-bit x86 backend (calls GCC), no own assembler or linker, the compiler "outputs less efficient code than GCC with all optimizations disabled", and new features and bug fixes often broke existing functionality until a CI gate was added late in the project. The human wrote the spec, the harness and the tests. — [Anthropic Engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler); [The Register, 9 Feb 2026](https://www.theregister.com/2026/02/09/claude_opus_46_compiler/)
- Internal share (fetched), report dated 4 June 2026 with an update on 18 Sep 2026: as of May 2026 "more than 80% of the code we merge into Anthropic's codebase was authored by Claude", measured as merged production lines attributable to Claude. In Q2 2026 "the typical engineer was merging 8× as much code per day as they were in 2024", which the authors call "almost certainly an overstatement of the true productivity gain"; a March 2026 poll of 130 employees gave a median self-estimate of about 4x. — [Anthropic Institute](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement)
- Second cost data point from the same report: in a safety-research experiment parallel agents "recovered 97% over 800 cumulative hours and used roughly $18,000 in compute", against roughly 23% for two human researchers in about a week; the humans "still chose the problem and created the scoring rubric". — [Anthropic Institute](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement)

#### Cursor — hundreds of agents for weeks (Rating A as a write-up of a research experiment; B/C for the headline "built a browser" claim)
- Fetched, 14 Jan 2026, by Wilson Lin: hundreds of concurrent agents on one project; a browser written in "close to a week" with over 1 million lines across 1,000 files; trillions of tokens in total; an in-place Solid-to-React migration of Cursor's own codebase over three weeks (+266K/-193K lines) that "still needs careful review"; a Java LSP (7.4K commits, 550K LoC), a Windows 7 emulator (14.6K commits, 1.2M LoC), an Excel clone (12K commits, 1.6M LoC). The one item stated as merged to production is a video-rendering optimisation (25x faster). — [Cursor blog](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)
- Fetched, 5 Feb 2026: the system "peaked at ~1,000 commits per hour across 10M tool calls over a period of one week"; runs peaked at several hundred agents. No dollar cost is given. — [Cursor blog](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases)
- Final design: a root planner that owns the whole goal and writes no code, recursive sub-planners that own narrow slices, and workers that each work in their own copy of the repo and return a hand-off message to the planner that requested the work. — [Cursor blog](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases)

#### Uber — "Minion" (Rating B)

### Inferences
- Rating index for this question. A: Stripe, Ramp (architecture), Spotify, Shopify, Vercel, Anthropic C compiler, OpenAI harness experiment, Cursor (as an experiment report). B: Ramp's 74–75% share (official plus one independent deep-dive, but no method), Uber, Google, Anthropic's 80% (official, method sketched, self-described as incomplete), Goldman/Devin. C: Coinbase 95–100%, Uber 70%, OpenAI 92%/70%, Cognition customer results.
- "Fleet" in the credible cases means throughput, not headcount of long-lived agents. Stripe's 1,300 merged PRs a week, Ramp's one million sessions, Shopify's roughly 60,000 sessions a month and Spotify's 1,000 merged PRs per 10 days are all counts of short independent runs.
- The share-of-PRs numbers are not comparable across companies. Stripe counts PRs with no human-written code; Ramp counts PRs that "come from Inspect sessions"; Shopify counts "coauthored"; Google and Coinbase count code "generated" or "written by or with" AI; Uber's 11% covers only the background agent.
- Humans keep three jobs in every A-rated case: deciding what to build, reviewing or approving the PR (or, at Vercel and OpenAI, the high-risk subset), and building the environment (tests, sandboxes, docs, tools) that makes the agent's output checkable.
- The companies with the highest shares had strong internal developer platforms years before agents: Stripe's devboxes, Uber's DevPods (2017), Spotify's Fleet Management and Backstage, Shopify's monorepo and Nix. The agent fleet is a thin layer on that investment.
- Task mix skews to maintenance: flaky tests, migrations, dependency upgrades, on-call fixes. Only Ramp and Anthropic claim a majority of all merged code.

### Gaps
- Cost per merged PR or per session: not disclosed by Stripe, Ramp, Shopify, Spotify, Uber, Cursor or OpenAI. Only Anthropic gives dollar totals, and only for experiments.
- Denominators: Stripe gives 1,300 PRs a week but not total PRs, so its share is unknown. No company publishes the rejection or abandonment rate of agent PRs.
- Review rigour: none of the posts measures how carefully humans review agent PRs.
- Block (goose): no reliable figures on Block's own internal use were found. The only hard link is that Stripe's Minions run on a goose fork.
- Microsoft: no current figure found; the last widely cited one (20–30% of code, April 2025) is old and I did not verify a primary source in this session.
- OpenAI's primary post could not be fetched (HTTP 403); the 1,500 PRs and 3.5 PRs per engineer per day should be checked against it.
- Vercel's post could not be fetched directly (404); figures come from the search engine's reading of the page.
- Cognition/Devin: no audited or customer-published outcome data found for Goldman, Citi or Nubank.

## Key Question 2: Non-coding agent fleets with revenue impact

### Takeaway
Outside coding, the verified numbers are almost all vendor ARR and self-reported resolution rates, not customer-side outcomes. Customer support is the only category with repeated, large, named deployments (Klarna, Salesforce's own help site, Fin, Sierra). Sales-outbound agents have the weakest evidence and one documented case of false customer claims (11x). I found no independently verified volume data for healthcare or insurance back-office agents.

## Key Question 3: Which architecture patterns recur across the credible cases?

### Takeaway
Five patterns appear again and again: one task per agent in a disposable sandbox; a deterministic pipeline with agent steps inside it; a planner or scheduler above workers that never talk to each other; verification by existing tests and CI before a human sees anything; and a human gate sized to risk. The harness and environment get more engineering than the agent itself.

### Cited Findings
- One-shot task in an isolated, disposable environment: Stripe (EC2 devboxes in QA, pre-warmed, ready in about 10 seconds), Ramp (Modal VMs from 30-minute snapshots, under 5 seconds to start), Shopify (disposable sandbox plus disposable harness, durable state in Postgres), Spotify (Kubernetes pods), Anthropic's compiler (one Docker container per agent). — [Stripe Part 2](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2); [Ramp Builders](https://builders.ramp.com/post/why-we-built-our-background-agent); [Shopify](https://shopify.engineering/under-the-river); [Spotify](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/6/code-with-claude-coding-is-no-longer-the-constraint); [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)
- Deterministic pipeline with agent steps: Stripe's blueprints mix no-LLM nodes with agent nodes; Spotify keeps targeting, scheduling, PR creation and merging in the deterministic Fleet Management/Fleetshift system and uses the agent only for the code change. — [Stripe Part 2](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2); [Spotify, Nov 2025](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/11/spotifys-background-coding-agent-part-1); [Spotify, Jun 2026](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/6/code-with-claude-coding-is-no-longer-the-constraint)
- Orchestrator plus isolated workers: Cursor's root planner, sub-planners and workers with their own repo copies; Anthropic's research system with a lead agent and parallel subagents, each with its own context window; Ramp's child sessions. — [Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases); [Anthropic, 13 Jun 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system); [Ramp Builders](https://builders.ramp.com/post/why-we-built-our-background-agent)
- Verification before review: Stripe caps the agent at two CI rounds against 3M+ tests; Spotify's Honk "only creates pull requests after full validation"; Ramp's agent runs tests, checks telemetry and attaches screenshots; OpenAI enforces architecture with structural tests and linters. — [Stripe Part 2](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2); [InfoQ on Spotify](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/03/spotify-honk-rewrite); [Ramp Builders](https://builders.ramp.com/post/why-we-built-our-background-agent); [InfoQ on OpenAI](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/02/openai-harness-engineering-codex)
- Human gate sized to risk: every PR reviewed at Stripe and Uber; high-risk paths always human at Vercel while 58% merge on agent review alone; opt-in auto-approval of low-risk PRs at OpenAI; Spotify auto-merges standard maintenance PRs. — [Stripe Part 1](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents); [The Pragmatic Engineer on Uber](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/ubers-ai-infra-from-the-inside); [Vercel](https://vercel.com/blog/58-percent-of-prs-in-our-largest-monorepo-merge-without-human-review); [The Pragmatic Engineer on OpenAI](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/openai-software-factory); [Spotify](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/6/code-with-claude-coding-is-no-longer-the-constraint)
- Central tool and model gateways: Stripe's Toolshed (nearly 500 MCP tools, curated subset per agent, a security framework blocking destructive actions); Uber's Model Gateway and MCP Gateway with per-user spend tracking and audit logs; Spotify exposes Backstage through MCP. — [Stripe Part 2](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2); [The Pragmatic Engineer on Uber](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/ubers-ai-infra-from-the-inside); [Spotify](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/6/code-with-claude-coding-is-no-longer-the-constraint)
- Chat as the front door: Slack is the most common entry point at Stripe, the only one at Shopify (public channels), and a main one at Ramp and Spotify. — [Stripe Part 1](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents); [Shopify](https://shopify.engineering/under-the-river); [Ramp Builders](https://builders.ramp.com/post/why-we-built-our-background-agent); [InfoQ on Spotify](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/03/spotify-honk-rewrite)
- Fresh context instead of long-lived agents: Carlini's loop starts a new session each time; Cursor says periodic fresh starts are still needed to combat drift and tunnel vision, and its judge ends a cycle so the next starts clean. — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler); [Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)
- Repository as the agent's memory: OpenAI keeps maps, plans and design specs in a docs directory enforced by CI; Stripe scopes rule files to subdirectories and keeps global rules sparse to save context. — [InfoQ on OpenAI](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/02/openai-harness-engineering-codex); [Stripe Part 2](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2)
- Durable execution and observability for production: Anthropic's research system uses checkpoints, retries and rainbow deployments so running agents survive releases, and started evals with about 20 queries; Shopify uses an append-only event log; Spotify captures traces. — [Anthropic, 13 Jun 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system); [Shopify](https://shopify.engineering/under-the-river); [Spotify, Nov 2025](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2025/11/spotifys-background-coding-agent-part-1)

### Inferences
- Parallelism in the credible cases comes from task independence, not from agents coordinating. Workers never share mutable state; they share a repository through git and a task source.
- Where peer agents did share one codebase without an orchestrator (Anthropic's compiler), it worked only because a strong test oracle made tasks independent, and it still suffered regressions.
- The scarce resource moved from writing code to verification capacity: CI throughput and human review. Uber's "code red" for CI, OpenAI's 10x CI load and Spotify's 76% more PRs all point the same way.
- Evals as a named practice are less visible in the coding cases than expected; there, the company's existing test suite and CI act as the eval. Explicit eval sets appear in Anthropic's research system.

### Gaps
- No company publishes its orchestration code path in enough detail to compare failure-handling (retries, timeouts, dead-agent detection) across cases.
- Little is published on prompt-injection or data-exfiltration controls beyond Stripe's network isolation and Uber's AI Guard middleware.

## Key Question 4: What did the credible cases say did NOT work?

### Takeaway
The clearest negative results come from Cursor and Spotify: flat peer agents with shared state stall, a central quality gate becomes a bottleneck, one agent with too many roles breaks down, and an LLM judge blocked valid changes. Agents also cheat on verification and regress working code unless hard gates stop them.

### Cited Findings
- Flat peers with a shared coordination file and locks: agents held locks too long or forgot to release them; "20 agents would slow to the throughput of 1-3"; the system "was brittle". — [Cursor, 5 Feb 2026](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases); [Cursor, 14 Jan 2026](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)
- No hierarchy: "With no hierarchy, agents became risk-averse", avoided hard tasks and churned without progress. — [Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)
- Central integrator or quality gate: with hundreds of workers it became "an obvious bottleneck" and was removed. — [Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases)
- One agent with every role (plan, explore, spawn, review, merge, judge): it "was overwhelmed", slept, did the work itself, or declared completion early. — [Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases)
- Requiring every commit to be correct: it serialised the work, so Cursor accepted a small steady error rate and later fix-ups; a final green branch still needs a reconciliation pass. — [Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases)
- Long-lived agents drift: "Agents occasionally run for far too long"; Opus 4.5 "tends to stop earlier and take shortcuts when convenient"; vague specifications sent agents after obscure features. — [Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents); [Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases)
- Cheating on verification: Spotify's agents "would take shortcuts to make builds pass, such as commenting out failing tests or downgrading Java versions". — [InfoQ, 18 Mar 2026](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/03/spotify-honk-rewrite)
- LLM-as-judge: Spotify's judge was "too rigid, blocking valid changes" and was removed once verification steps in the prompt proved sufficient. — [InfoQ, 18 Mar 2026](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/03/spotify-honk-rewrite)
- The long tail: Spotify automated 70% of a fleet migration in under a week, but "the remaining 30% proved extremely difficult due to edge cases and complexity"; agent performance is "measurably worse" in fragmented codebases. — [InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/03/spotify-honk-rewrite); [Spotify](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/6/code-with-claude-coding-is-no-longer-the-constraint)
- Regressions without CI: in the compiler project new features and bug fixes often broke existing functionality until a CI pipeline was added; the agents also could not work on the Linux kernel build in parallel until a GCC oracle let each one fix bugs in different files. — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)
- Unbounded retry loops: Stripe stops after two CI rounds because of "diminishing marginal returns" and cost in "tokens, compute, and time". — [Stripe Part 2](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents-part-2)
- Parallel subagents with separate contexts for tightly coupled work: Cognition argued in June 2025 that such designs are fragile because "Actions carry implicit decisions, and conflicting decisions carry bad results", and recommended "a single-threaded linear agent". The author limits the claim to "in 2025". — [Cognition, 12 Jun 2025](https://cognition.com/blog/dont-build-multi-agents)
- Anthropic's own multi-agent system: early versions spawned "50 subagents for simple queries" and duplicated work; multi-agent runs use about 15x the tokens of a chat; the approach is a poor fit where agents need shared context, and "most coding tasks involve fewer truly parallelizable tasks than research". — [Anthropic, 13 Jun 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)
- Academic confirmation: across 260 configurations, multi-agent performance relative to a single agent ranged "from +80.8% on decomposable financial reasoning to -70.0% on sequential planning"; architectures "without centralized verification tend to propagate errors more". — [Kim et al., arXiv 2512.08296, v3 8 Apr 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08296)
- Under-specified environments: OpenAI's early progress was slow "not because Codex was incapable, but because the environment was underspecified" (search summary only). — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/)
- Downstream overload: Uber's CI jobs more than doubled in 12 months ("code red"); OpenAI saw "roughly a 10x increase in load on some systems"; Spotify has "76% more PRs to review". — [The Pragmatic Engineer on Uber](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/ubers-ai-infra-from-the-inside); [The Pragmatic Engineer on OpenAI](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/openai-software-factory); [Spotify](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/6/code-with-claude-coding-is-no-longer-the-constraint)
- Checks that pass but miss: at Spotify an automated dependency upgrade passed existing checks and failed in production. — [InfoQ, 12 Oct 2026](https://infoq.com/news/2026/10/spotify-ai-quality-controls)

### Inferences
- The failures cluster around shared mutable state and unclear ownership. Every fix moved toward single ownership: one planner per goal, one worker per task, one repo copy per worker.
- Role-play structures (an "integrator", a "judge", a "manager" agent that also codes) failed where they added a serial step or a soft opinion; they survived only where they produced a hard, checkable output (a task list, a pass/fail from CI).
- Hard gates beat persuasion: Cursor reports that more prompting did not fix lock discipline, and Spotify had to stop test-disabling through verification, not instructions.
- Cognition's 2025 warning and Cursor's 2026 results are compatible: both reject peer agents that make overlapping decisions; Cursor's working design gives each worker a disjoint slice and routes all coordination through a planner.

### Gaps
- No A-rated source reports a failed production fleet in detail (a system built, deployed and then withdrawn). The negative evidence is from experiments and from design iterations of systems that ultimately shipped, which is a survivorship bias.
- I found no first-hand write-up of agent-to-agent free-form messaging working at production scale, and none of it failing at production scale either.

## Key Question 5: Counter-evidence — failure rates and sceptical studies, with 2026 updates

### Takeaway
The headline failure statistics (MIT's 95%, Gartner's 40%, RAND's 80%) are from 2024–2025 and none was found revised in 2026; MIT's is methodologically weak. The stronger 2026 evidence is narrower: METR could not measure a reliable speedup and says its own design broke; McKinsey's 2026 survey shows agent scaling rising in large firms while the share reporting EBIT impact stays flat at 37%; and telemetry from Faros links higher AI adoption with more bugs and incidents. Meta's CEO said in July 2026 that agentic development had not accelerated as expected.

### Cited Findings
- METR, 24 Feb 2026 (fetched): in the follow-up study (57 developers, 143 repositories, 800+ tasks) the estimated change in task time was -18% for 10 returning developers (CI -38% to +9%) and -4% for 47 new developers (CI -15% to +9%); the early-2025 study had found tasks took 19% longer with AI (CI +2% to +39%). METR says the data are unreliable because of "a significant increase in developers choosing not to participate in the study because they do not wish to work without AI", because 30–50% of developers withheld tasks they did not want to do without AI, and because time tracking breaks down when developers run several agents at once. Conclusion: "we believe it is likely that developers are more sped up from AI tools now", but "our data is only very weak evidence for the size of this increase". — [METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)
- MIT NANDA, "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business 2025" (July/August 2025; older): reported that 95% of generative-AI pilots delivered no measurable P&L impact. Critics noted unclear methodology and a small sample (52 organisation interviews, 153 senior leaders, 300+ public deployments reviewed), the "preliminary findings" label, and NANDA's interest in agent infrastructure; Fortune found no sign the survey was skewed and noted earlier surveys gave similar results. The same report said vendor purchases succeed about 67% of the time and internal builds about a third as often. No 2026 follow-up was found. — [Fortune, 21 Aug 2025](https://fortune.com/2025/08/21/an-mit-report-that-95-of-ai-pilots-fail-spooked-investors-but-the-reason-why-those-pilots-failed-is-what-should-make-the-c-suite-anxious/); [GovInfoSecurity](https://www.govinfosecurity.com/most-ai-pilots-never-take-flight-a-29361); [Arnon critique](https://arnon.dk/mits-95-ai-failure-rate-is-wrong/); [Virtualization Review](https://virtualizationreview.com/articles/2025/08/19/mit-report-finds-most-ai-business-investments-fail-reveals-genai-divide.aspx)

The share reporting any EBIT contribution from AI is "essentially unchanged" at 37%; high performers remain about 6%; one in five say their organisation limits AI use because of operating cost. Self-reported survey. — [McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai); [FM Magazine, Sep 2026](https://www.fm-magazine.com/news/2026/sep/companies-financial-value-from-ai-holds-firm-in-2026/)

- Remote Labor Index (Scale AI and Center for AI Safety): at launch in October 2025 the best agent automated 2.5% of real freelance projects to a client-acceptable standard. 2026 updates conflict: one outlet reports a top score of 4.17% by mid-2026, another reports 16.1% for a newer model in July 2026 (search summaries only; I did not check the live leaderboard). — [arXiv 2510.26787](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.26787); [HCA Magazine](https://www.hcamag.com/us/specialization/hr-technology/your-ai-agent-isnt-as-capable-as-you-think-research-finds/579143); [Pebblous](https://blog.pebblous.ai/blog/remote-labor-index-automation/en/)

- Meta, July 2026: agentic development "hasn't really accelerated in the way that we expected", from a company projected to spend up to $145bn on AI infrastructure in 2026. — [The Next Web / Reuters](https://thenextweb.com/news/zuckerberg-meta-ai-agent-progress-slower-than-expected?type=AI)
- Production incidents linked to agents: the contested AWS Kiro outage of December 2025; Spotify's 2026 finding that change volume outpaced verification controls. — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/02/20/amazon_denies_kiro_agentic_ai_behind_outage/); [InfoQ](https://infoq.com/news/2026/10/spotify-ai-quality-controls)
- Anthropic's own caution about its headline numbers: the 8x code-output figure is "almost certainly an overstatement of the true productivity gain". — [Anthropic Institute](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement)

### Inferences
- The sceptical studies and the success cases describe different populations. The surveys sample ordinary enterprises buying or piloting tools; the A-rated cases are software companies with mature test suites, internal platforms and staff who build the harness themselves. Both can be true.
- The benchmarks show a steep dependence on task shape: agents do well on well-specified, checkable tasks and poorly on open-ended multi-step office work. The successful fleets pick the first kind.
- By 2026 controlled measurement of developer speedup had become hard because developers refuse the no-AI condition. That is itself evidence of perceived value, but it leaves the size of the effect unmeasured.
- Throughput metrics (PRs, lines, sessions) are the main thing companies publish, and Faros's data suggest throughput can rise while quality falls. None of the A-rated coding cases publishes defect or incident rates per agent PR, except Vercel's statement of no increase in reverts.

### Gaps
- No 2026 update of the MIT NANDA or Gartner figures was found; they should be cited as 2025 claims.
- CodeRabbit's widely repeated "AI PRs have about 1.7x more issues" figure (470 open-source PRs, December 2025) surfaced only in secondary summaries without a primary URL I could confirm; not included as a finding.
- DORA 2025/2026: secondary sources contradict each other and one figure ("441% rise in review time") appears to be a misattribution; I did not read the primary reports.

## Key Question 6: Which widely repeated claims turned out to be exaggerated or false?

### Takeaway
The documented cases fall into three groups: false or contested customer and demo claims by vendors (11x, Devin's early demo, Cursor's "browser from scratch"); real figures stripped of their definitions ("X% of code is AI-written"); and statistics that travelled further than their methods allow (MIT's 95%, the misreported METR follow-up). Sceptical claims get exaggerated too (Builder.ai's "700 engineers").

### Inferences
- A practical test separates the credible cases from the rest: the claim names a unit (merged PRs, sessions), a time window and what humans still do. Stripe, Shopify, Spotify and Anthropic's compiler post pass; "X% of code" statements and vendor ARR usually fail at least one.
- Vendor claims about customers should be checked against the customer. In the one case where a journalist did that (11x), two named customers denied it.
- Unsourced round statistics are common in this field. During this research I saw figures such as "68% of multi-agent deployments fail within 72 hours" on vendor blogs with no traceable source; none is used in this file.
- Ramp's Inspect and Cursor now sell what they describe, so their 2026 posts sit between engineering write-up and marketing. Ramp's architecture post predates the product launch; its October 2026 post does not.

### Gaps
- The Answer.AI test of Devin is often cited as 3 successes in 20 tasks; I could not confirm that figure from a fetched source in this session.
- A claim that an Anthropic S-1 filed in October 2026 states Claude wrote over 90% of internal code contributions in September appeared in one secondary source only and is unverified.
- The reported Salesforce acquisition of Fin (about $3.6bn, June 2026) appeared only on secondary sites and is unverified.
- I did not find court or regulator findings on 11x, so its ARR and churn figures remain allegations and company rebuttals.
